# build a district-level summary we can reuse for all questions
district_results <- g24_cng |>
  filter(CDDIST != 0) |>             # drop statewide total row (CDDIST == 0)
  group_by(CDDIST) |>
  summarise(
    dem_votes = sum(CNGDEM01, na.rm = TRUE),
    rep_votes = sum(CNGREP01, na.rm = TRUE),
    ind_votes = sum(CNGIND01, na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  mutate(
    total_votes = dem_votes + rep_votes + ind_votes,
    dem_share = dem_votes / (dem_votes + rep_votes),
    rep_share = rep_votes / (dem_votes + rep_votes),
    winner_party = if_else(dem_share > rep_share, "DEM", "REP"),
    margin = abs(dem_share - rep_share)
  )

Answer 1

Using the district-level summaries, Democrats win 43 of the 52 congressional seats (about 82.7%), while Republicans win 9 seats (about 17.3%).

Statewide, however, Democrats receive about 60.7% of the two-party vote for Congress, and Republicans receive about 39.3%. So Democrats win a noticeably larger share of seats than their share of the vote, while Republicans win fewer seats than their vote share would suggest.

Answer 3

Across districts, the Democratic two-party vote share has:

a median around 63%,

an interquartile range from roughly 52% to 69%, and

extremes ranging from 0% (solid Republican districts) to 100% (uncontested Democratic districts).

The histogram shows that many districts are not very competitive: a large share of districts are strongly Democratic, a smaller group are strongly Republican, and only a handful fall near the 50–50 range. This pattern suggests a map with many “safe” seats and relatively few true swing districts.
